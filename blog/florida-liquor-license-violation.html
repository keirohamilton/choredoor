Violation Defense

What to do if you get a Florida liquor license violation

By Keith Hamilton · Captain (Ret.), Florida ABT May 20, 2026 7 min read

A notice from the Division of Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco lands on your desk. Maybe an agent conducted a compliance check, maybe an employee sold to a minor, maybe a complaint was filed. Whatever the trigger, the license your entire business depends on is now at risk — and what you do in the first days matters enormously.

For 33 years I was on the other side of that notice. I know how these cases are built, what moves the needle, and what mistakes make a bad situation worse. Here is how to think clearly when it happens to you.

First: understand what you're actually facing

Most alcohol violations in Florida are handled administratively by the division — not as criminal matters, though some incidents can involve both. The state typically issues an administrative complaint or a notice describing the alleged violation. Depending on the severity and your history, the potential penalties include:

  • Fines — often tied to the type and number of violations
  • Suspension — a temporary loss of your ability to sell alcohol
  • Revocation — permanent loss of the license in the most serious cases
  • Compliance conditions — required training, monitoring, or corrective steps

The specific exposure depends heavily on the facts, your prior record, and how the case is handled from here. Two businesses with the "same" violation can end up in very different places.

What NOT to do

The instinct to make the problem go away fast is exactly what turns a manageable case into a costly one. Avoid these:

  • Don't ignore the notice. Deadlines are real, and missing one can forfeit your right to contest the charge entirely.
  • Don't admit or "explain" informally. Casual statements to an investigator can become evidence. Be respectful, not talkative.
  • Don't sign anything you don't fully understand. A settlement or waiver signed too early can lock in a penalty you could have reduced or avoided.
  • Don't assume it's hopeless. You have rights and options — but only if you use them in time.
The outcome is rarely decided by the violation itself. It's decided by how the case is handled from the moment the notice arrives.

Your options for responding

Once you receive an administrative complaint, you generally have a window to respond and to elect how your case proceeds. Broadly, that can mean:

Requesting a hearing

You can dispute the allegations. Where the key facts are contested, that can lead to a formal hearing; where they aren't, an informal proceeding may resolve the legal questions. Choosing the right track is a strategic decision, not a formality.

Negotiating a resolution

Many cases resolve through settlement. The difference between a good settlement and a bad one comes down to how well your position is presented — your record, your corrective actions, and the specific circumstances that put the outcome in context.

Mounting a defense

Not every allegation holds up. Procedures may not have been followed, evidence may be weak, or the facts may support a far lighter outcome than the complaint suggests. Someone who knows how these cases are constructed from the inside can find the openings others miss.

Why Inside Experience Matters

A violation case is shaped by process, precedent, and the discretion of the people handling it. Having spent decades as a Captain within the division, I understand how these decisions are actually made — which is exactly the knowledge that turns a defense from guesswork into strategy.

The best defense starts before the violation

The businesses that weather a violation best are the ones that prepared for the possibility. Documented Responsible Vendor training, clean records, and sound day-to-day compliance practices are not just good operations — they are evidence that a single incident was an exception, not a pattern. That evidence is often what tips a case toward a warning or reduced penalty instead of a suspension.
